Probability

Events, variables, and densities

Definition: Density and mass

A discrete random variable \(X\) has a probability mass function \(p(x)\ge 0\) with \(\sum_x p(x)=1\). A continuous random variable on \(\mathbb{R}^d\) has a density \(p(x)\ge 0\) with \(\int p(x) dx=1\). The density is not a probability. \(p(x)\) can exceed \(1\). The probability of a set \(A\) is \(\int_A p(x) dx\).

Intuition

Think of a density as height and a probability as area. A very narrow bump can be very tall and still have area one. This is why, later, the flow density \(p_\theta(x)\) at an integer pixel is not the probability of that pixel.

The joint density \(p(x,z)\) describes the pair. The marginal of \(x\) sums or integrates the other variable out, \[p(x)=\int p(x,z) dz.\] The conditional is the joint divided by the marginal, when the marginal is positive: \[p(z\mid x)=\frac{p(x,z)}{p(x)},\qquad p(x,z)=p(x\mid z) p(z).\] The second display is the chain rule. It is the only legal way to build a joint from conditionals on a directed graph: each node is conditioned on its parents, and the graph is acyclic.

Result: Bayes

\[p(z\mid x)=\frac{p(x\mid z) p(z)}{p(x)}.\] The denominator is the marginal likelihood. In a latent-variable model it is the integral that the ELBO avoids computing.

Expectation, variance, and Jensen

Definition: Expectation

For a function \(f\) and a density \(p\), \[\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p}[f(x)]=\int f(x) p(x) dx,\] when the integral exists. Linearity holds even if the coordinates of \(x\) are dependent: \(\mathbb{E}[a^\top x]=a^\top\mathbb{E}[x]\).

Variance is \(\mathrm{Var}(f)=\mathbb{E}[f^2]-(\mathbb{E} f)^2\). For a sum of independent random variables the variances add. They do not add when the variables share randomness. That fact is the whole of the importance-sampling variance story: a bad proposal makes \(f p/q\) wildly dependent on the draw.

Result: Jensen

If \(\psi\) is concave and the expectations exist, then \(\psi(\mathbb{E}[X])\ge \mathbb{E}[\psi(X)]\). The logarithm is concave, so \[\log \mathbb{E}[X]\ge \mathbb{E}[\log X].\] Equality holds when \(X\) is almost surely constant, or more generally when \(\psi\) is affine on the range of \(X\).

Intuition

Jensen is the reason a lower bound appears every time a logarithm moves inside an expectation. Lecture 4, slide 34, writes exactly this for \(\psi=\log\). Homework 2 uses it twice: once for the ELBO, and once for variational dequantization.

Practice: A one-line Jensen gap

Let \(Z\in\{0,1\}\) with equal probability, and let \(f(0)=1\), \(f(1)=e\). Compare \(\log\mathbb{E}[f(Z)]\) with \(\mathbb{E}[\log f(Z)]\).

Worked solution

\(\mathbb{E}[f]=(1+e)/2\), so \(\log\mathbb{E}[f]=\log(1+e)-\log 2\). And \(\mathbb{E}[\log f]=\frac12\log 1+\frac12\log e=\frac12\). The gap is \(\log(1+e)-\log 2-\frac12>0\) because \(f\) is not constant.
