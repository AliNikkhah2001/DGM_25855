Matrix calculus and Jacobians

Generative models differentiate scalars with respect to vectors and matrices. This chapter fixes the conventions and derives the four derivatives the homeworks need: a linear form, a quadratic form, a log-determinant, and a Jacobian determinant.

Layout

A scalar \(L(\theta)\) has a gradient \(\nabla_\theta L\) that is a column vector of the same shape as \(\theta\). The differential is the inner product \[dL=\langle \nabla_\theta L, d\theta\rangle.\] If \(\theta\) is a matrix, the inner product is the Frobenius product \(\langle A,B\rangle=\operatorname{tr}(A^\top B)\). This is the layout used below. It matches the chain rule in coordinates.

Result: Linear and quadratic forms

Let \(a\in\mathbb{R}^d\) be constant and let \(A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) be constant. Then \[\begin{aligned} \nabla_x (a^\top x)&=a,\\ \nabla_x (x^\top A x)&=(A+A^\top)x. \end{aligned}\] If \(A\) is symmetric, the second gradient is \(2Ax\). In particular \(\nabla_x \|x\|^2=2x\) and \(\nabla_x \frac12\|x\|^2=x\).

Intuition

The factor \(2\) is the usual one from \((x^2)'=2x\). People drop it when they differentiate \(\frac12 x^\top \Sigma^{-1}x\), because the \(\frac12\) cancels the \(2\). Gaussian exponents are written with that \(\frac12\) for this reason.

Trace and log-determinant

Result: Derivatives in the matrix

For constant \(B\) and invertible \(A\), \[\begin{aligned} \nabla_A \operatorname{tr}(A^\top B)&=B,\\ \nabla_A \log|\det A|&=A^{-\top}. \end{aligned}\] The second identity is the matrix calculus behind Glow. If \(W\) depends on a diagonal vector \(s\) only through \(W=\operatorname{diag}(s)\), then \[\frac{\partial}{\partial s_i}\log|\det W|=\frac{1}{s_i} \quad\text{when }s_i\neq 0,\] and the gradient of \(\sum_i\log| s_i|\) is the vector \((1/s_i)\).

A short derivation of the log-determinant: \(\det(A+dA)=\det(A)\det(I+A^{-1}dA)\), and \(\det(I+H)=1+\operatorname{tr}(H)+o(\|H\|)\). Therefore \[d\log|\det A|=\operatorname{tr}(A^{-1} dA)=\langle A^{-\top}, dA\rangle.\]

Jacobians and the change-of-variables factor

Let \(f:\mathbb{R}^D\to\mathbb{R}^D\) be continuously differentiable and invertible, with differentiable inverse, and with \(J_f(u)\) invertible at the points we use. If \(u\) has density \(p_0\) and \(y=f(u)\), then \(y\) has density \[ p(y)=p_0(f^{-1}(y)) |\det J_{f^{-1}}(y)| =p_0(u) |\det J_f(u)|^{-1}.\] In logarithms, with \(u=f^{-1}(y)\), \[\log p(y)=\log p_0(u)-\log|\det J_f(u)|.\]

Intuition: Why the absolute value

A reflection \(f(u)=-u\) preserves probability but has Jacobian \(-1\). Densities are nonnegative, so the volume factor is the absolute value of the determinant. On a triangular matrix the determinant is the product of the diagonal entries, which is why coupling layers are easy.

Result: Triangular determinant

If \(L\) is lower triangular with ones on the diagonal, \(\det L=1\). If \(U\) is strictly upper triangular, then \(U+\operatorname{diag}(s)\) is upper triangular with diagonal \(s\), and \(\det(U+\operatorname{diag}(s))=\prod_i s_i\). A permutation matrix \(P\) has \(\det P=\pm 1\). Hence if \(W=PL(U+\operatorname{diag}(s))\), \[\log|\det W|=\sum_{i=1}^{C}\log| s_i|.\]

Practice: A \(2\times 2\) Jacobian

Let \(f(u_1,u_2)=(u_1, u_2+u_1^2)\). Compute \(J_f\), its determinant, and the density of \(y=f(u)\) when \(u\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_2)\).

Worked solution

\[J_f(u)=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\ 2u_1&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad \det J_f=1.\] The map is a shear. It preserves volume, so \(\log p(y)=\log p_0(f^{-1}(y))\). The inverse is \((y_1, y_2-y_1^2)\), and \[p(y)=(2\pi)^{-1}\exp\left(-\frac12 y_1^2-\frac12(y_2-y_1^2)^2\right).\] This is the map in Homework 2, problem 3.2. It will return there as a counterexample to a diagonal-Gaussian restriction.
